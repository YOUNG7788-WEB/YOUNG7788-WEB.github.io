Building my A-share quant research process

2026-11-14 · 8 min read

A first-person account of strategy rules, data audits and simulated backtests.

I started looking into A-share quant trading for a simple reason. I wanted to spend less time chasing headlines or changing my mind in the middle of a session and put stock selection, entries, exits and risk controls into rules I could check repeatedly. It sounded like putting trading on autopilot. The first thing I actually had to do was check whether the fuel gauge was broken 😅.

This is about strategy research, data troubleshooting and simulated backtests I did with Claude and Codex. The returns are historical simulations of research portfolios. They aren't returns from my own live account. I spent much more time asking whether a number was reliable than picking indicators that sounded impressive.

Turning “looks good” into rules.

We organized the original ideas and compared the shorter swing strategy A with the medium-term strategy B under the same constraints. It wouldn't make sense to keep one fully invested and the other mostly in cash, then just declare the one with the smaller drawdown the winner. What you assume after a pause matters, too: staying out indefinitely and allowing trading to resume can produce very different results.

We focused on B, which screened for quality and then waited for a trend signal. These were the parameters frozen for the experiment. They're here to show exactly what we tested.

  1. Start with ordinary A shares on the Shanghai and Shenzhen main boards that have at least 250 valid trading days since listing. Handle historical ST designations, delisting periods, suspensions and unknown status as they stood at the time.
  2. Require weighted-average ROE based on net income attributable to shareholders of the parent of at least 10% in each of the last three published annual reports. The latest annual report must show positive operating cash flow per share and a debt-to-assets ratio no higher than 70%. Average daily turnover over the last 20 valid sessions must be at least 50 million yuan.
  3. For the market filter, compare the CSI 300 close from the last completed session with its 60-day simple moving average. Consider new positions only when the close is strictly above it. Missing data doesn't pass the filter.
  4. The stock must close strictly above the highest close of the previous 60 valid sessions and above its 120-day moving average. Volume must be at least the previous 20-day average, and the actual gain must be under 7%. Neither lookback window includes the current session.
  5. A signal confirmed at the close can only be considered the next trading day. Check trading status, the reference price and an executable quote. You can't look at the opening price and pretend you bought at it in advance.

Writing down the rules forced us to answer questions we'd been able to gloss over. Is a breakout strictly greater than the threshold, or can it equal it? Does the moving average include today? Do we stop or keep going if index data is missing? Those small differences change trades.

Deciding how much to buy can be harder than picking what to buy.

B used a nominal risk budget of 0.4% of equity per trade and a nominal stop of 10%, implying a target position of roughly 4%. The caps were 6% per stock, 15% per industry and six holdings. Cash, fees and lot sizes also applied. If the budget couldn't buy one lot, we skipped the trade.

The exit rules were fixed too. A close 10% or more below the corporate-action-adjusted purchase cost, a close below the 60-day moving average, or a holding period of 120 valid trading days generated an exit candidate for the next session. A 10% stop sets a rule for generating a signal. Gaps, suspensions, limit-down moves and failed fills can make the actual loss worse.

At portfolio level, a 10% drawdown from the high-water mark paused new entries. It did not liquidate positions or cap losses. We would review after at least 20 full trading days before deciding whether to resume. One backtest scenario assumed that each pause ended with permission to resume. That was an assumption for comparing rules, separate from any actual authorization.

Downloading data is a long way from having usable data.

The first audit found 3,378 raw market files. Of those, 3,328 passed structural checks and 50 were quarantined. Another six tickers had no downloaded data and were listed separately. Some quarantined files contained nonpositive prices. We didn't turn negatives into zeros or fill holes with another security's data to make the test run.

We added checks for columns, date order and duplicates, finite positive prices, reasonable high and low prices, and valid volume and turnover. A bad cache should produce a clear error. New downloads go to a temporary file, get read back and validated, and only then replace the live file. Each run records the input sources, scope and hashes so we can tell whether two runs used the same data.

Passing structural checks only tells us the table hasn't obviously fallen apart. Volume units, price adjustments, filing revisions and historical eligibility can still skew results. We identified a data implementation that handled volumes by ticker prefix. A cross-check of three securities across eight security-days found volume figures nearly 100 times apart. We blocked that confirmed problem path, tried the next source and kept the old files. A handful of samples didn't justify multiplying every cached volume by 100.

A financial statement's year doesn't tell you when it was known.

A report available from an API today may have been corrected or restated. A date field may not be the day its figures first became public. Using today's version in the past gives the simulated trader a peek at the answer.

We compared versions for the same reporting period and checked whether assets, liabilities and equity reconciled. Some differences were real. To show that any one trade changed, we would still need the first disclosure date, whether the difference crossed a strategy threshold, and whether the other conditions were met. We kept the raw data, candidate versions and audit reports for further investigation.

B+: would a 40-day breakout get us in earlier than a 60-day breakout?

We changed just the breakout window and compared B60 with B+40. In the November 2 run, the looser rule improved only one of three historical windows. The most recent window went from around -3.67% to -4.17%. Higher commissions and slippage did not improve the case for B+.

That test didn't establish that the change would make more money. What was approved afterward was forward shadow observation using two virtual ledgers. At least 20 full trading days gives us time to check the process and records. It doesn't unlock an achievement called “consistently profitable.”

ATR and momentum didn't give me quite the results I wanted either.

On November 6 we tested baseline B, sizing changed to use ATR alone, and volatility-adjusted momentum ranking alone. Three historical windows plus a high-cost scenario in the most recent window made 12 combinations. Changing the two features separately let us tell what each did.

The ATR version tried buying less of more volatile stocks. The momentum version divided the 60-day gain by a volatility measure and reordered the same candidates. The indicator library also implemented 38 price and volume indicators. We marked financial, valuation and order-book fields as missing when reliable data wasn't available.

The latest standard scenario ran from January 3, 2023 through October 27, 2026. Each run started with 100,000 yuan in simulated capital. These are total returns for that period and maximum drawdowns, without annualizing returns.

  • Baseline B: total return about -3.69%, maximum drawdown about -11.19%.
  • ATR sizing only: total return about -4.31%, maximum drawdown about -11.36%.
  • Volatility-adjusted momentum ranking only: total return about +5.50%, maximum drawdown about -9.19%.

Under higher costs, momentum's total return dropped to about +1.89%. It passed a research screen set in advance and ATR didn't. Momentum did have a worse drawdown in 2019–2022 and a higher average allocation than baseline in the latest window. After breaking down returns by year and block-resampling the observed daily returns, the estimated difference still crossed zero. If a positive number made me reach for the champagne, the rest of that report made me put it back.

We've already seen this historical data. Changing a parameter doesn't turn it into a new exam. The results still have limits around the timing of financial statements, historical eligibility and adjusted execution-price proxies. The +5.50% is worth following up on and is no guarantee of future returns.

What I keep after a run.

I care more now about being able to review the whole process. Freeze the inputs and parameters, generate candidates, simulated fills, cash and daily equity, then read back the CSV files and calculate return, drawdown and cash independently. I don't want a function signing off on its own work.

The project includes reproduction commands. The B/B+ comparison requires local daily bars, financial statements and index data, with a new output directory that doesn't already exist. These are research scripts from the project, not standalone trading software.

python research/compare_bplus_20261102.py --data-root "YOUR_HISTORICAL_DATA_DIRECTORY" --output "NEW_RESEARCH_OUTPUT_DIRECTORY"
python research/test_bplus_signal_20261102.py
python research/test_bplus_shadow.py

For the newer parameter run we also reconstructed 11,139 daily equity records. We handled historical stamp duty by date instead of applying today's costs all the way back. For the reduction by half starting August 28, 2023, we used the announcement from the Ministry of Finance and the State Taxation Administration ↗. These checks can catch implementation errors. They can't rescue unreliable source data.

I put the tested parameter sets, periods, simulated capital and limitations into a public research summary ↗ to keep the runs straight. My actual holdings, account records and credentials stay private.

What has changed for me.

I haven't built a money machine. I'm much more hesitant to say a strategy is reliable. More signals don't automatically help, and fewer trades don't automatically mean better risk management. A runnable backtest, reproducible arithmetic, usable data and executable trades are different hurdles.

I'm still checking data and observing the rules. New research doesn't automatically replace the existing execution rules, and any live action needs separate confirmation. I used to ask, “How much can it make?” Now I'm more likely to ask whether the program could be very diligently calculating the wrong answer. It isn't as exciting, but it helps me fool myself a little less.
