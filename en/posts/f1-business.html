As F1 makes more money, is racing still center stage?

2026-11-14 · 3 min read

What fans, brands and hosts pay for, and what that business owes the people who keep racing safe.

On this page ＋
Start reading

After the Singapore Grand Prix, I went back to a question I'd been thinking about since watching the Shanghai race earlier this year: now that F1 is this popular, who's paying for what?

I've watched F1 for years, and of course I still watch for the racing. A single lap can have more plot than a season of television. Pan the camera outside the paddock and there's another show: celebrities, socialites, brand parties and expensive hospitality tickets. It feels like a giant social event travelling around the world with the cars. The K-pop stars in Singapore and entertainers in Shanghai show up with their own stories and fans. The cars do laps, and everyone else gets the trending topics going.

I don't know how much each guest knows about racing, and I'm not going to hand out quizzes. As a fan, it can feel odd when the most expensive, visible spots look like a social scene. From an organizer's point of view, though, visitors don't all have to discuss tire strategy. Someone comes for their favorite star, buys a ticket, watches a broadcast or buys merchandise, and F1 has another way to bring people in. It's great if they discover the racing, but the attention is already generating revenue either way.

Someone has to feed this expensive machine. Power units, chassis development, team operations and moving equipment between continents all cost money. Hosting fees, broadcast rights, tickets, sponsorship and merchandise keep the global operation going. I used to wonder if a niche audience could no longer pay for it and the broader public had to pick up the bill. That was too sweeping, but my central impression holds: F1 is increasingly good at making a race into a global entertainment business that gives more people different things to spend on.

Audi and Cadillac are both on the 2026 team list. My view is that besides the racing, they see what this platform can do for visibility and how people perceive their brands. Fans remember the names and potential buyers may give them another look. Actual sales figures have to come from the results. I can't fill in a company's accounts from my sofa. Here's the FIA 2026 team guide ↗ so the dates don't drift into paddock rumor.

I also thought of the China GT race-car fire at the same Shanghai circuit, and the discussions I saw about what track staff were paid. A figure of 60 yuan circulated online. I didn't find enough first-hand evidence to treat it as verified pay. What I care about is whether the people who respond to emergencies and keep everyone safe get training, equipment and compensation that match their responsibilities as the business grows.

A smaller event is no excuse to skimp on safety. When a car catches fire, the people running toward it can't put it out with enthusiasm alone. Stars, sponsors and hospitality suites help light up the show, but safety staff aren't scenery at the edge of the frame. Profiting from the exposure while pinching pennies backstage doesn't sit right with me.

Attention can help sustain F1. I don't expect it to go back to being a pastime for a small circle. More fans are welcome, and resources help teams put on good races. I hope the cars stay in the middle of the stage as it grows, and that the people who keep the race going have a place there too. It would be a shame to spend the whole day on the spectacle and find the race itself mattered least 🤔.
